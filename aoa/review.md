# Legal Review: Articles of Association — British Violin Making Association Limited

**Document reviewed:** `aoa/aoa.md`
**Date of review:** 8 February 2026 (updated with external feedback)
**Governing law:** Companies Act 2006 (England & Wales); company limited by guarantee

---

## 1. Statutory Non-Compliance

### 1.1 EGM Notice Period Below Statutory Minimum (Article 13 — HIGH)

Article 13 specifies **seven days' written notice** for Extraordinary General Meetings. Section 307(1) of the Companies Act 2006 requires a minimum of **14 clear days' notice** for all general meetings of a private company. The articles cannot reduce this statutory floor. Any EGM called on seven days' notice would be invalid unless the short-notice consent procedure under s.307(5)–(6) is separately followed (requiring consent of members holding at least 90% of voting rights).

**Recommendation:** Amend Article 13 to specify a minimum of 14 clear days' notice, or at least 14 days, to match the statutory requirement. Consider whether to include an express short-notice consent provision.

### 1.2 No Provision for Poll Voting (Article 14 — MEDIUM)

Article 14(2) provides only for a "show of hands" with a Chairman's casting vote. Under s.321 of the Act, members have a statutory right to demand a poll at any general meeting. The articles cannot exclude this right (s.321(2)). While the Act provides the fallback, the articles should positively provide for poll voting procedures to avoid confusion and ensure legitimacy of contested votes.

**Recommendation:** Add provisions for when and how a poll may be demanded, and how votes on a poll are counted.

### 1.3 Written Resolutions Not Addressed (MEDIUM)

Sections 288–300 of the Act give private companies the right to pass ordinary and special resolutions as written resolutions without a meeting. The articles are silent on this. While the statutory provisions apply by default, express articles provisions would provide clarity, particularly regarding the circulation process and lapsing period.

**Recommendation:** Consider adding an article acknowledging and/or regulating the use of written resolutions.

## 2. Objects and Powers

### 2.1 Objects May Be Too Narrowly Drawn (Article 4 — MEDIUM)

Article 4(a) refers to "violin and bow making" specifically. The British Violin Making Association's members may in practice also make violas, cellos, double basses, and other stringed instruments. The objects as drafted could be interpreted to exclude these activities, potentially constraining the Company's ability to serve its full membership.

The broader term **"lutherie"** (the craft of making and repairing stringed instruments) would encompass the full range of work undertaken by members without limiting the Company's scope.

**Recommendation:** Consider replacing "violin and bow making" in Article 4(a) with broader language, e.g., "lutherie, including but not limited to the making and repair of violins, violas, cellos, and bows" — or a similarly inclusive formulation. This should be reviewed against the actual scope of the membership's activities to ensure the objects clause is neither too narrow nor too broad for mutual trading purposes.

## 3. Governance Structure — Dual Authority Risks

### 3.1 Board vs Management Committee Tension (HIGH)

The articles create an unusual dual governance structure: the Management Committee "formulates policy, strategy, and activities" (Art 8(4)) while the Board of Directors "implements" those policies (Art 6(4)/8(4)). The Board may refuse to implement MC decisions only on narrow legal grounds (Art 6(4)(a)–(c)).

This creates several risks:

- **Deadlock:** If the Board refuses an MC directive on legal grounds, there is no dispute resolution mechanism — no mediation, no member vote to break the deadlock, no independent adjudication.
- **Accountability gap:** Directors owe statutory duties to the *company* under s.171–177 of the Act, not to the Management Committee. Article 6(4) attempts to navigate this but the tension between the MC's directive authority and the directors' duty of independent judgment (s.173) could produce genuine legal conflict.
- **Board reconstitution risk:** Since the MC appoints Directors (Art 7(1)), a frustrated MC could attempt to remove and replace Directors who exercise their veto, undermining the very safeguard the articles try to create.

**Recommendation:** Add a dispute resolution procedure (e.g., independent legal advice, member vote, or mandatory mediation) for situations where the Board declines to implement an MC recommendation. Consider whether the veto grounds in Art 6(4) are sufficiently broad.

### 3.2 No Removal Mechanism for Directors (HIGH)

The articles cover appointment (Art 7) but contain **no provision for removing a Director**. Section 168 of the Act provides that members can remove a director by ordinary resolution on special notice, but this requires a full general meeting — a heavy process for a small association.

There is no provision for:
- The Management Committee removing a Director it appointed
- Automatic vacation of office on defined events (bankruptcy, mental incapacity, disqualification, prolonged absence, resignation)
- What happens if a Director ceases to be a member of the Management Committee

**Recommendation:** Add a comprehensive "Vacation of Office" article, listing the circumstances in which a Director ceases to hold office. Also consider giving the MC express power to remove a Director by resolution.

### 3.3 Board Meeting Procedures Absent (MEDIUM)

There are no provisions governing:
- Notice of Board meetings
- Frequency of Board meetings
- Who chairs Board meetings (the MC Chairman may not be a Director — see Art 8(1)(a))
- Quorum for Board decisions (beyond the implied minimum of the 3-director floor in Art 6(1))
- Voting at Board level, including casting vote
- Written resolutions of the Board

**Recommendation:** Add an article covering Board meeting procedures, or state that the Model Articles for private companies limited by guarantee (Schedule 2, SI 2008/3229) apply as a fallback for matters not covered.

### 3.4 "Normally" Creates Ambiguity (Article 7(2) — LOW)

Article 7(2) says the Chairman, Secretary, and Treasurer "shall normally be appointed as Directors." The word "normally" is legally imprecise. It is unclear what triggers the exception to "normally" beyond the situations in Art 7(3). This could lead to disputes about whether an officer has a legitimate expectation of appointment.

**Recommendation:** Replace "shall normally be" with "shall, subject to Article 7(3), be" for precision.

## 4. Membership

### 4.1 No Admission Process Defined (MEDIUM)

Article 9(1) says Full Membership is "open to individuals who support the aims of the Association and have paid the required subscription" but does not specify:
- Who decides on admission (Board? MC? Automatic on payment?)
- Whether there is an application process
- Any right to refuse admission, and on what grounds

Article 9(2) says Honorary Members are "invited by the Association" without specifying who acts for the Association in extending the invitation.

**Recommendation:** Add provisions specifying the admission process for each class, including who has authority to admit or refuse applicants.

### 4.2 Associate Members as Statutory Members — Unintended Consequences (MEDIUM)

Article 9(3)(b) explicitly states Associate Members are "Members of the Company for the purposes of the Articles and the Companies Act." This means:

- They count towards the **quorum** of 20 at General Meetings (Art 14(1)), even though they cannot vote. In theory, a General Meeting could be quorate with 20 Associate Members and zero Full Members — but no resolution could pass.
- They have standing to bring an **unfair prejudice petition** under s.994 of the Act.
- They are entitled to receive **copies of accounts** and all statutory member communications.
- They participate in **winding-up distributions** under Article 18(3).
- Their names appear on the **register of members** open to public inspection.

Some of these consequences may be unintended. If the intention is merely to give Associate Members access to events and information, a contractual licence (not statutory membership) may be more appropriate.

**Recommendation:** Carefully consider whether Associate Members should be statutory members. If so, consider whether their quorum and winding-up rights are intended. If not, redefine them as contractual affiliates rather than Companies Act members.

### 4.3 Organisations as Associate Members (Article 9(3) — LOW)

Article 9(3) permits "individuals or organisations" as Associate Members. If an organisation is a statutory member, it must provide the £1 guarantee (Art 3). The articles should specify how an organisation exercises its membership rights (e.g., through an authorised representative).

**Recommendation:** Add a provision requiring corporate/organisational members to appoint a named representative.

### 4.4 No Voluntary Resignation Mechanism (LOW)

There is no article allowing a member to resign voluntarily. Article 10 covers only termination *by the Committee*. Under general principles, a member of a CLG can resign by giving notice, but an express article would avoid doubt and specify any formalities (written notice, effective date, whether unpaid subscriptions remain due).

**Recommendation:** Add a provision for voluntary resignation from membership.

### 4.5 Disciplinary Process Is Thin (Article 10(3) — MEDIUM)

Article 10(3) allows the Committee to require a member to resign if their conduct is "prejudicial to the interests of the Association." The process is:
1. Membership "reviewed"
2. Member "invited to comment"
3. Committee "may require them to resign"

There is no right to a hearing, no right of appeal, no defined standard of proof, no requirement for the decision to be in writing, and no defined timeframe. This is vulnerable to challenge on natural justice grounds, particularly since membership of the company carries statutory rights.

**Recommendation:** Strengthen the disciplinary procedure: require written notice of the complaint, a reasonable time to respond, a fair hearing (or at least a written submissions process), a reasoned decision in writing, and a right of appeal to a general meeting or independent person.

### 4.6 Member Guarantee — Administrative Burden (Article 3 — MEDIUM)

Under s.11(3) of the Act, a company limited by guarantee must have a statement of guarantee as part of its constitution. Each person who becomes a member undertakes to contribute up to £1 on winding up. In practice, the Company will likely need **each member to sign a written confirmation of this guarantee** — either as part of the application process or as a standalone document.

This represents a substantial administrative task, particularly for:
- **Existing members** of the former unincorporated association who are transitioning to Company membership
- **Overseas members**, where obtaining wet-ink signatures or verified electronic signatures may be difficult and slow

**Recommendation:** Design the membership application form to incorporate the guarantee undertaking as a standard clause, so it is captured at the point of admission. For existing members transitioning from the unincorporated association, consider a bulk confirmation process (e.g., a written notice to all members with a return form, or an electronic consent mechanism if the articles/rules permit electronic communications). Take advice on whether the Act requires individual signed forms or whether a less formal confirmation suffices.

## 5. Meetings and Voting

### 5.1 Quorum of 20 May Be Impractical (Article 14(1) — MEDIUM)

A quorum of 20 members is high for a specialist association. If total membership is, say, 50–80, consistently achieving quorum may be difficult — particularly for EGMs on short notice. There is also **no provision for what happens if a meeting is inquorate**: no adjournment procedure, no reduced quorum on reconvening.

**Recommendation:** Consider whether 20 is appropriate given current and projected membership levels. Add an adjournment provision with a reduced quorum (e.g., "if no quorum is present within 30 minutes, the meeting shall be adjourned to the same day, time and place the following week, at which the quorum shall be [X] members").

### 5.2 Proxy Voting Mentioned but Not Regulated (Article 14(1) — MEDIUM)

Article 14(1) counts members "present in person or by proxy" towards quorum, but the articles contain no provisions governing:
- The form of a proxy appointment
- The deadline for lodging proxy forms
- Whether a proxy may speak and/or vote
- Whether a proxy must be a member
- Revocation of proxy appointments

Sections 324–331 of the Act provide default proxy rights, but without articles provisions there will be practical uncertainty about procedure.

**Recommendation:** Add comprehensive proxy provisions or adopt by reference the relevant Model Article provisions.

### 5.3 No Adjournment Provisions (LOW)

Apart from the narrow provision in Art 15(6) (electronic connection failure), there are no general adjournment provisions for meetings.

**Recommendation:** Add standard adjournment provisions covering both inquorate meetings and voluntary adjournment by the Chair.

### 5.4 No Chairman of General Meetings Provision (LOW)

There is no article specifying who chairs a General Meeting. Presumably it is the MC Chairman, but what if they are absent, or there is a conflict of interest? There is no provision for electing an alternative chair.

**Recommendation:** Add a provision: "The Chairman of the Management Committee shall preside at General Meetings. If the Chairman is absent or unwilling, the meeting shall elect a chair from among the members present."

### 5.5 Rigid October AGM Requirement (Article 12(1) — LOW)

Article 12(1) mandates the AGM "in October of each year." This is unusually rigid. If circumstances prevent an October meeting (pandemic, natural disaster, insufficient quorum), the articles provide no fallback. Note: the Act itself does not require private companies to hold AGMs (s.336 applies only to public companies), so this is a self-imposed obligation.

**Recommendation:** Consider softening to "no later than 15 months after the previous AGM" or "in October of each year, or within such further period as the Management Committee may determine (not exceeding three months)."

## 6. Financial and Mutuality Provisions

### 6.1 Strict Ban on Non-Member Contributions (Article 5(c) — MEDIUM)

Article 5(c) says the Company "shall decline contributions from non-members to strictly preserve its status as a mutual trading entity." This is presumably aimed at HMRC's mutual trading rules (whereby mutual surpluses are not subject to corporation tax). However, the drafting is potentially over-broad:

- Does "contributions" include payment for goods or services (e.g., sale of publications to non-members, non-member attendance at public events)?
- Could this inadvertently prevent the company from accepting sponsorship or donations for specific activities?

The mutual trading exemption depends on the *character* of the income, not a blanket ban. HMRC looks at whether the surplus arises from transactions with members for the mutual benefit.

**Recommendation:** Consider refining the language to distinguish between (a) member subscriptions and mutual trading income (which preserves the exemption) and (b) incidental non-member income (which may be taxable but need not be prohibited). Take specialist tax advice.

### 6.2 "Administrator" Role Undefined (Article 16(1) — MEDIUM)

Article 16(1) refers to the "Treasurer and/or Administrator" but "Administrator" is not a defined term anywhere in the articles. It is unclear who this person is, how they are appointed, what authority they hold, or what their relationship is to the Board or Management Committee. This is the only mention of this role in the entire document, yet it is given authority to handle the Company's money.

**Recommendation:** Either (a) add a definition of "Administrator" to Article 1 and an article governing their appointment, role, and accountability; or (b) remove the reference and vest the duty solely in the Treasurer with an express power to delegate to a named person approved by the Management Committee.

### 6.3 Bank Account Should Be in Name of the Company (Article 16(1) — MEDIUM)

Article 16(1) requires monies to be paid into "a current financial account in the name of the Association." As an incorporated entity, the account must be in the name of the **Company** ("British Violin Making Association Limited"), not the informal trading style. Banks will require the registered company name for a corporate account. Using "the Association" here is legally imprecise and could cause practical difficulties when opening or operating the account.

**Recommendation:** Amend Article 16(1) to refer to an account "in the name of the Company" rather than "in the name of the Association."

### 6.4 Tension Between Art 17(1) and Art 17(3) (LOW)

Article 17(1) says income "shall be applied solely towards the benefit of the Members." Article 17(3) says no portion "shall be paid... by way of profit to the members." These two provisions sit in tension and would benefit from a connecting clause explaining that "benefit" means collective services and activities, not individual financial distributions.

**Recommendation:** Add a clarifying sentence, e.g., "For the avoidance of doubt, 'benefit of the Members' in this Article means the provision of services, activities, and facilities to the Members collectively in pursuance of the Objects, and does not include any distribution of income or property to individual Members."

## 7. Winding Up (Article 18)

### 7.1 Formatting Error (HIGH — Drafting)

Article 18 is a single run-on paragraph. Sub-articles (1), (2), and (3) are concatenated without line breaks, making the article difficult to read and cross-reference. Every other article in the document uses proper paragraph separation.

**Recommendation:** Reformat Article 18 with proper paragraph breaks for each sub-article.

### 7.2 Who Should Share in Winding-Up Distributions? (Article 18(3) — MEDIUM)

Article 18(3) distributes Mutual Fund surplus to "Full Members, Honorary Members, or Associate Members at the time of dissolution, in proportion to their contribution." Several issues arise:

**Honorary Members:** Honorary Members pay no subscriptions (Art 9(2)). Their "contribution" in financial terms is zero. It is questionable whether they should share in a distribution of mutual funds that were built up from other members' subscriptions. If "contribution" is interpreted financially, Honorary Members would receive nothing anyway — but the article's intent is ambiguous. If the intention is to reward long service or distinction, this should be stated explicitly; if Honorary Membership is purely an honour, they arguably should not participate in financial distributions.

**Associate Members:** Associate Members have no voting rights (Art 9(3)) and therefore no say in the dissolution decision itself. Including them in the distribution creates an asymmetry: they bear the consequences of decisions they cannot influence.

**"In proportion to their contribution":** This phrase is vague. Does it mean:
- Total subscriptions paid over the lifetime of membership?
- Years of membership (equal weight per year)?
- Equal shares regardless of tenure?
- Something else?

This ambiguity would likely produce disputes at the worst possible time — when the Company is being wound up.

**Recommendation:** (a) Decide as a matter of policy whether Honorary Members and Associate Members should share in winding-up distributions — there is a reasonable argument that only subscribing Full Members should benefit. (b) Define "in proportion to their contribution" precisely (e.g., "in proportion to the total subscriptions paid by each Member" or "in equal shares among all eligible Members"). (c) Consider a simpler alternative: equal distribution among Full Members only.

## 8. Administrative Protections

### 8.1 Conflicts of Interest — Only Covers Proposed Transactions (Article 19 — LOW)

Article 19 requires disclosure of interests in "a proposed transaction or arrangement." It does not cover:
- Existing transactions or arrangements (s.182 of the Act requires disclosure of these too)
- Situational conflicts that are not transaction-specific (e.g., a Director who is also a Director of a competing organisation)

**Recommendation:** Extend Article 19 to cover existing transactions and general situational conflicts, consistent with s.175, s.177, and s.182 of the Act.

### 8.2 Indemnity Provision Is Narrow (Article 20 — LOW)

Article 20 only indemnifies Directors and officers in proceedings where judgment is given in their favour, they are acquitted, or the court grants relief. This is the minimum permitted by the Act. It does not cover:
- Costs of defending proceedings that are settled (rather than concluded by judgment)
- Third-party claims (s.234 permits qualifying third-party indemnity provisions)
- Management Committee members who are not Directors

**Recommendation:** Consider extending the indemnity to the fullest extent permitted by s.232–235 of the Act, and explicitly covering Management Committee members.

### 8.3 No Provision for Directors' and Officers' Insurance (LOW)

There is no article authorising the company to purchase D&O insurance. While s.233 of the Act permits this regardless of the articles, an express provision provides comfort to those serving.

**Recommendation:** Add a provision authorising the company to purchase and maintain insurance for Directors, Management Committee members, and officers.

### 8.4 Borrowing Powers — Are They Appropriate? (Article 21 — MEDIUM)

Article 21 grants the Directors broad powers to "borrow money and to mortgage or charge its undertaking, property and assets" and "issue debentures and other securities," subject only to Management Committee approval.

For a small mutual association of craftspeople, these powers are unusually broad. The power to mortgage property, issue debentures, and act as guarantor for third-party debts (Art 21(1) — "any debt, liability or obligation... of any third party") is standard boilerplate for commercial companies but may be disproportionate to the BVMA's nature and scale. The risks include:

- **Personal exposure for the MC:** If the MC approves borrowing that the Company cannot repay, MC members who approved it could face scrutiny, even though their personal liability is capped at £1.
- **Third-party guarantees:** The power to grant security for "any third party" obligations is particularly concerning — there is no obvious reason a mutual violin making association should guarantee anyone else's debts.
- **Asset risk:** If the Company acquires property (e.g., a workshop or archive), borrowing powers allow it to be mortgaged, potentially putting collectively-owned assets at risk.

**Recommendation:** Consider one of the following approaches:
1. **Remove Article 21 entirely** if borrowing is not anticipated, relying on future amendment by special resolution if the need ever arises.
2. **Restrict the powers significantly**, e.g., cap borrowing at a defined amount (subject to AGM approval), prohibit third-party guarantees entirely, and prohibit mortgaging or charging Company property without a special resolution of the Members.
3. **At minimum**, delete the third-party guarantee language ("or of any third party") from Article 21(1) and require member approval (not just MC approval) for borrowing above a de minimis threshold.

### 9.1 Company Secretary vs Association Secretary (MEDIUM)

The articles refer to a "Secretary" as one of the three officers of the Management Committee (Art 8(1)(a)), elected annually at the AGM. This is the traditional *association secretary* — an administrative role focused on correspondence, agendas, and minutes.

However, a **company secretary** under the Companies Act is a distinct role with specific legal responsibilities, including:
- Filing annual confirmation statements and other returns at Companies House
- Maintaining statutory registers (members, directors, PSC)
- Ensuring compliance with filing deadlines
- Custody of the company seal (if any) and statutory books

Private companies are not required to appoint a company secretary (s.270(1)), but if the BVMA chooses to do so — and for good governance it probably should — the person appointed needs to understand these statutory duties. The MC Secretary elected at the AGM may not be the right person for this role; they may lack the technical knowledge or time.

**Recommendation:** (a) Add an article distinguishing between the MC Secretary (the association's administrative officer) and any Company Secretary appointed under the Act. (b) If a Company Secretary is to be appointed, specify who appoints them (the Board, since it is a statutory compliance role), their qualifications or duties, and their term of office. (c) Consider whether this role should be filled by someone with relevant experience — potentially a professional or a specific named individual — given the importance of diligent compliance with Companies House requirements.

## 10. Drafting and Consistency Issues

### 10.1 Inconsistent Terminology: "Company" vs "Association" (MEDIUM)

The document uses "Company" and "Association" interchangeably (e.g., Art 8(1) says "Association", Art 8(4) says "Company", Art 10(3) says "Association", Art 16(1) says "Association"). Since the entity is a company, **"Company"** should be used consistently throughout the constitutional document. "Association" should be reserved for the informal trading style defined in Art 2(2) and should not appear in operative provisions.

This is not merely a stylistic point. When the articles refer to "the Association" in an operative clause, it creates ambiguity about whether the obligation or power attaches to the *Company* (the legal entity) or to some looser concept of the association. In a dispute, this could matter.

**Recommendation:** Conduct a global find-and-replace, changing "the Association" to "the Company" in all operative articles, while retaining "Association" only in Art 2(2) (trading style), Art 4(e) (historical reference), and any other purely descriptive context.

### 10.2 "Member" Not Capitalised Consistently (Multiple Articles — MEDIUM)

"Member" is a defined term in Article 1. Defined terms should be capitalised throughout to signal that the defined meaning applies. However, "member" appears in lowercase in numerous operative provisions:

- Article 3: "each member" (four occurrences)
- Article 7(3): "another member of the Management Committee"
- Article 8(6): "fully subscribed member"
- Article 10(2): "a member is one month or more in arrears"
- Article 10(3): "a member's conduct" / "The member shall be invited"
- Article 11(2): "any member's promotional material" / "one is a member"
- Article 16(2): "one other member appointed by the Committee"

When "member" is lowercase, it is ambiguous whether the defined meaning (which includes all three classes — Full, Honorary, and Associate) is intended. For instance, in Art 16(2), does "one other member" mean a Member of the Company or simply another person appointed by the Committee?

**Recommendation:** Capitalise "Member" wherever it refers to a person as defined in Article 1. Where a different meaning is intended (e.g., a member of the Management Committee as distinct from a Member of the Company), use "member of the Committee" or similar disambiguating language.

### 10.3 "Fully Subscribed Member" Undefined (Article 8(6))

Article 8(6) refers to "any fully subscribed member" — this term is not defined in Article 1. Presumably it means a member whose subscription is paid up, but this should be explicit.

### 10.4 Inconsistent Subscription Authority (Articles 9(3)(a) vs 10(1))

Article 9(3)(a) says Associate Member subscriptions are "determined by the Management Committee." Article 10(1) says subscriptions are "proposed by the Management Committee and approved by the AGM." It is unclear whether Associate Member subscriptions require AGM approval or are solely within the MC's discretion.

### 10.5 Article 11 Is Policy, Not Constitution

Article 11 (Use of Logo and Title) reads more like an operational policy or membership rule than a constitutional provision. Including detailed guidance on business cards and social media in the articles means that any amendment requires a special resolution (75% majority). This is inflexible for what may need to be updated regularly.

**Recommendation:** Move the substance of Article 11 to a separate Code of Conduct or Membership Rules that the Management Committee can update by ordinary resolution, retaining only a brief empowering article in the AOA (e.g., "The Management Committee may make rules governing the use of the Company's name, logo, and marks by Members").
